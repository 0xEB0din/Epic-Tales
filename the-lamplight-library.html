Carried across two thousand years, from the banks of the Nile to the deserts of Arabia and the gardens of Andalusia.

حَيّ بن يَقْظان
A Boy Raised by a Gazelle
An Andalusian tale of the 12th century · and, it turns out, a true one
I

The Island

Long ago, beyond the trade winds and the known coasts, there was an island. It lay on the equator, where the seasons were so gentle that the trees never lost their leaves and the fruit never lost its sweetness. No people had ever walked its shores. The animals there did not know fear.

This is where the story takes place. But it is not where the boy begins. The boy begins on another island, far away, in trouble.

II

The Crossing

On the larger island to the east there lived a princess. She had married in secret, against her brother's wishes, and when she gave birth to a son she knew her brother would not let the child live. So she did what mothers have always done when the world refuses to make room for their love — she made the room herself. She wrapped the baby in soft cloth, laid him in a sealed wooden box, and gave him to the tide.

The sea, that night, was kind. The current carried the box gently westward — past the reefs, past the dawn — until it came to rest on the shore of the empty island.

The baby cried.

III

The Gazelle

In the grove above the shore, a gazelle was grieving. Her fawn had been taken by an eagle some days before, and her body still ached with the milk she had no one to give. When the wind carried the cry of the child to her ears, she did not hesitate. She did not stop to wonder. She ran.

She found him on the sand — this strange small thing, pink and trembling, smelling nothing like a fawn. But she lowered her nose to his cheek, and he turned toward her, and his small hand closed around the soft fur of her throat. And in that moment, somewhere beyond reason, she became his mother.

She named him by lying down beside him.

IV

A Childhood Without Words

The boy grew up among the gazelles. His mother taught him which leaves were good and which to leave alone. She taught him to hear the rustle that means danger and the rustle that means rain. When he tried to walk on four legs and could not, she watched him patiently until he discovered, on his own, the secret of his two.

He learned the language of the herd — the soft snorts of greeting, the sharper calls of warning. He never spoke a human word. He did not know there were human words to speak.

One day he saw, in the still water of a pool, that he was different. He had no horns, no fur, no swiftness of foot. He wept. Then he wove himself a cloak of leaves, tied branches to his hands like little hooves, and went back to her. She rested her nose against his shoulder. Whatever shape he was, he was hers.

V

The Quiet Question

In time, the gazelle grew old. She moved more slowly through the grass. The boy — now a young man — slowed his step to match hers, brought her the softest leaves, and slept beside her so she would not be cold.

One morning, she did not wake.

He sat down beside her and stayed there a long time. Something had left her. He could see it without seeing it: the body of his mother was still here, but his mother was not. Where had she gone? What was the difference between a body that breathes and a body that does not? What had he loved all these years, if not the warm shape lying still before him?

These were his first questions. They were the seed of everything that followed.

VI

The Sky Opens

After his grief had quieted, the boy began to look around him as if for the first time. He learned to make fire, and loved it — the only thing on the island that seemed alive the way he was, moving and hungry and warm, always reaching upward.

And at night he looked up. The stars wheeled overhead in their slow, faithful circles, the same patterns returning season after season, never colliding, never lost. Such order, he thought, does not happen by accident. Someone must be holding it — the way his mother had once held him.

Alone on his island, with no teacher and no book, with nothing but his own watching and wondering, the boy reasoned his way toward a quiet certainty: that behind the turning stars and the reaching fire and the love of a gazelle for a child not her own, there was something vast and gentle and good. He had been held all along.

VII

Not Alone

The boy became a man, and the man grew wise — not from being told things, but from looking closely and loving what he saw. He lived in peace with the island, and the island with him.

And then, one day, far across the water, a small boat appeared. In it was another person — the first the man had ever seen. They had no language in common, only patience and goodwill, and slowly they came to understand one another. The man learned, at last, that he was not the only one of his kind. He had a mother who chose him, a sky that held him, and now a friend who found him.

This story is real. A doctor and philosopher named Ibn Tufayl wrote it in Muslim Spain more than eight hundred years ago, to argue that a person alone, with nothing but their own eyes and heart, could find their way to truth. Centuries later it was translated into Latin and English — and it helped inspire a book you may know: Robinson Crusoe.

Some children arrive in a sealed box on a strange tide. And still the world bends down, like a gazelle in the dark, and decides to love them.

قيس وليلى
Qays & Layla
The most famous love story in Arabic — and, when you look closely, the strangest
I

The Boy We Are Not Sure Existed

Here is the first honest thing to say: we do not entirely know if Qays was real. There was, most likely, a young poet of that name in the Najd — the high desert of central Arabia — sometime around the year 680. A handful of aching verses survive under his name. But the story everyone tells about him was assembled, piece by piece, over hundreds of years, by storytellers who never met him and rarely agreed with one another.

They do not even agree on how it began. One early version says Qays and Layla were cousins who grew up together, tending the same flocks, in love before they understood the word. Another says he saw her once, as a grown man, and was undone in a single glance.

So read what follows the way the desert told it: as something half true, which is often truer than fact.

تَعَلَّقَ روحي روحَها قبلَ خَلقِناومِنْ بعدِ ما كُنّا نِطافًا وفي المَهدِ
My soul clung to her soul before we were made,
and after — when we were drops of water, and again in the cradle.
attributed to Qays ibn al-Mulawwah
II

The Name He Could Not Stop Saying

What we can say is this: Qays became a poet, and he could not stop saying her name. He said it in the marketplace. He said it to his camel. He said it to other poets, who said it back to him, until the whole desert was passing Layla's name from mouth to mouth like a coin.

In that world, this was not romantic. It was a scandal. A woman's name belonged inside her family's tents. To carry it out, set it into rhyme, and let strangers sing it across the peninsula was to shame her household — and a shamed household could not then hand the girl to the man who shamed it. Everyone knew this. Qays, a poet, knew it best of all.

And he never stopped.

III

The Refusal He Built Himself

When Qays at last went to Layla's father and asked to marry her, the answer was no. Of course it was no. He had spent years making it impossible — and her father, in any case, had already promised her to a man from a distant tribe. Layla was sent away across the sands.

And here is where the story turns strange, and more interesting than the version usually told. Because some who have studied Qays for a lifetime believe he was never really trying to reach Layla at all. A poet of his gifts knew exactly what his verses would cost. It is hard not to suspect that, somewhere beneath knowing, he chose the locked door over the open one. That what he loved was not the girl he might have married and grown old beside — but the ache of wanting her, which could last forever, and never disappoint him.

There is a whole school of old Arabic love-poetry, the 'Udhri tradition, built on exactly this: a love kept pure precisely because it is never fulfilled. The longing is the love. To get what you want would be to lose it.

وَما حُبُّ الدِيارِ شَغَفنَ قَلبيولَكِنْ حُبُّ مَنْ سَكَنَ الدِيارا
It is not these dwellings that have seized my heart,
but love of the one who once lived within them.
attributed to Qays ibn al-Mulawwah
IV

In Love With the Longing

He walked out into the open desert and did not come back. He lived among the wild animals — the gazelles especially, because the poets always compared a beloved's eyes to a gazelle's, and he could not bear to be far from anything that reminded him of her. He grew thin. Travelers who stumbled on him knew him only by his verses, which were the most beautiful anyone had heard. People began to call him Majnun — not quite "madman," but something closer to taken over, emptied of everything but one thing.

And the legend keeps a detail that gives the whole game away. In more than one telling, Qays is offered the very thing he supposedly wants — a meeting with Layla, a chance to be near her, in some versions even her hand once she is free. And he turns away. He will not close the distance. A real Layla, who could be tired or ordinary or simply his, was no match for the perfect Layla he carried in his head, who could never be lost because she had never quite been had.

V

The Prayer Nobody Agrees On

His father, desperate, took him on the long pilgrimage to Mecca, hoping the holiest place on earth might cure him. At the great shrine, the old man told his son to lift his hands and pray for the love to be lifted from his heart.

And here the story shatters into pieces, because no two storytellers have ever agreed on what Qays actually prayed. You will find all of these, told with equal certainty:

one telling

"Lord, heal me of her. Lift this love away."

another

"Lord, do not lessen it — make my love for Layla a hundred times what it is."

a third

"And if I pray — will it bring me Layla? Then I will pray for that, and nothing else."

Perhaps the disagreement is the truest thing in the whole story. A man who loved the longing itself would, in the end, be impossible to pin down — even on the question of what he wanted. His father, every version agrees on this much, went home alone.

“To see Layla,
you must borrow my eyes.”
— Qays, when told the woman he loved was not, to others, especially beautiful
VI

What Survived

The legend ends the way legends like this must. Layla, married and far away and faithful to no one but a memory, died first. Qays was found not long after, out among his animals, gone. Whatever he had been chasing, he never had to catch it. The longing stayed perfect to the last.

But the poems did not die. They travelled by mouth across Arabia, into Persia, into India and the courts of the Mughals, into the songs of Sufi mystics who read the whole tale as a parable — Qays was the soul, Layla was God, and the unbearable distance between them was the whole point of being alive. In the twelfth century the poet Nizami spun it into one of the great epics of the world. Eight hundred years after that, a guitarist named Eric Clapton read a translation, and wrote a song you have heard.

Maybe that is why this story refuses to die, even when you tell it honestly — especially when you tell it honestly. Everyone has wanted something they arranged never to have. Everyone has, at least once, loved the wanting. Qays just never pretended otherwise.

In Arabic there is a quiet way of measuring a love: how long, after the lovers are gone, the verses keep being said.

By that measure — real or not, wise or not — Qays and Layla have been loved for fourteen hundred years.

It belongs now to you, too — to keep, or to argue with, whichever you like.

عَنْتَرَة وعَبْلَة
Antarah & Abla
A true story of pre-Islamic Arabia · the warrior-poet who would not stay a slave
I

The Boy They Would Not Name

This story is true, and we are sure of the man at its center — which makes it rarer than most. His name was Antarah ibn Shaddad, and he was born, around the year 525, into a tribe that did not want him.

His father was an Arab chieftain of the Banu Abs. His mother, Zabiba, was an Ethiopian woman the tribe had enslaved in a raid. By the cruel arithmetic of that world, a child took the mother's station — so Antarah was born a slave, dark-skinned among people who prized pale lineage, and his own father would not claim him. The tribe could not even be bothered to use his name. They called him boy. They called him you, there.

But there was no hiding what he was. By nine he could lift a full-grown sheep onto his shoulders and carry it home. He grew into the strongest man anyone had seen, and — quietly, where no one was listening — the finest poet too.

II

Abla

He loved his cousin Abla. He had loved her since they were children, and when he became a poet there was only ever one subject worth the trouble. He made her into verse so fine that, fourteen centuries later, lovers across Arabia still borrow his lines on the night they propose.

But Antarah was a slave, and Abla was the freeborn daughter of his uncle. He could carry her water and guard her people's camels. He could not, by any law the tribe recognized, ask for her hand. There was a wall between them, and it was not made of stone — it was made of who the world had decided he was.

III

“Defend Your Tribe, and You Are Free”

Then one day raiders fell on the Banu Abs and began driving off the herds and the people. In the chaos, Antarah's father shouted at him to charge and fight. And Antarah — who had been a slave his whole life, and knew exactly what he was worth to them — called back the bitter truth: A slave does not know how to charge. A slave only knows how to milk the goats and serve.

His father understood the bargain being demanded of him. And he answered with the seven words that turned the whole story: "Defend your tribe, O Antar — and you are free."

Antarah charged. The legends say the raiders broke against him like water on rock, and the Banu Abs won back everything they had lost. When it was over, the boy who had no name was a free man — and the men who had called him you, there learned, at last, to call him Antarah.

وَلَقَد ذَكَرتُكِ وَالرِماحُ نَواهِلٌ مِنّيوَبيضُ الهِندِ تَقطُرُ مِن دَمي
I remembered you — while the spears drank deep of me,
and the bright Indian swords ran with my blood.
Antarah ibn Shaddad · from his Mu'allaqa
IV

The Impossible Bride-Price

Free, and a hero, Antarah went to his uncle and asked for Abla. But the tribe was not done making him earn what others were simply given. They set a bride-price meant to be impossible: a herd of rare and famous camels, the prized asafir she-camels, kept far to the north in the realm of a king.

It was a quest designed to kill him, or at least to send him away forever. Instead he rode north, took the camels through danger that became its own legend, and drove them home across the desert to lay them down before his uncle's tent. Whatever the tribe could invent, Antarah simply did — and made a poem of it on the way back.

V

The Name the Desert Kept

And so the slave married the free woman, and the boy with no name became the most celebrated knight in Arabia. His war-poem was counted among the seven greatest odes ever composed — the Mu'allaqat, the "hanging poems," said to have been written in gold and hung at the holy shrine in Mecca for all to read.

Think of what that means. The tribe that would not say his name hung his words in the holiest place they knew. The poems he had made in the dark, where no one was listening, outlived every man who had ever called him boy.

Fourteen hundred years later, children in Arabia still learn his story. His lines are still recited at weddings. And on the night they mean to ask the most important question of their lives, lovers still reach, without quite knowing why, for the words of a man who was told he was no one — and who answered with a sword, a poem, and a love he refused to be talked out of.

Qays loved a door he kept locked. Antarah found a door the whole world had locked against him — and broke it down.

The boy they would not name became the name the desert never forgot.

رودوبيس
The Egyptian Cinderella
The oldest Cinderella in the world · written down on the Nile two thousand years ago
I

The Oldest Cinderella in the World

You already know this story. Or you think you do — the lost slipper, the search, the girl no one expected, lifted out of the ashes and made a queen. You learned it as a child, in a version with a glass slipper and a fairy godmother and a clock striking midnight.

But that version is young. Long before the French told it, and the Germans, and everyone after — more than a thousand years before — there was an older Cinderella. It is the oldest anyone has ever found, and it was not set in a European castle. It was set here, in Egypt, on the banks of the Nile, and a Greek traveller named Strabo wrote it down two thousand years ago, insisting it was true. Perhaps some of it was. Egypt was already unthinkably old even then — its great pyramids had stood for a thousand years before this story begins — a narrow green country strung along one slow river that ran up the middle of an endless desert, where everything that lived, lived because of the water.

There is no pumpkin in it, and no fairy godmother, and no midnight. The magic, such as it is, comes from a great green river, a kind and sleepy old man, and a bird. This is how the first Cinderella goes.

II

The Girl Who Came Down the River

Her name was Rhodopis, which in the Greek means rosy-cheeked, and she was not Egyptian at all. She had been born far across the sea, in green and rocky Greece, and while she was still a child, pirates had taken her — snatched off a beach, bound, carried over the water, and sold in a foreign market like a sack of grain. The man who bought her was rich and lived near Naucratis, a busy trading city on one of the mouths of the Nile, and there she grew up, in a country whose language she had to learn one hard word at a time.

Everything about her marked her out. Her hair was the colour of wheat where everyone else's was black as the river at night. Her eyes were green where everyone else's were brown. Her fair skin burned and freckled under the same sun that the other girls only deepened in. She was a stranger to the bone, and the other servant girls of the house never let her forget it. To them she was the pale foreign thing who talked wrong, and they made her days small and sharp.

III

Her Only Friends Had Wings

Her master was a kind man, which is to say he was too sleepy to be a cruel one. He spent his days dozing in the cool of his courtyard and rarely saw what happened beneath his own roof. So he never noticed how the other girls fed Rhodopis the heaviest, dirtiest work and saved the easy hours for themselves; how she ground the grain and hauled the water and scrubbed the floors while they sat in the shade and whispered. She did not complain. There was no one to complain to.

So she found her friends elsewhere. There was a mischievous monkey who stole figs from the kitchen and brought them to her, and a great drowsy hippopotamus who watched her from the green reeds with patient eyes, and — most of all — the birds. She was so gentle and so still that the river birds learned to trust her, coming down to take grain from her open palm, settling on her shoulders as if she were a branch. They asked her nothing and judged her nothing; to a bird it does not matter where a girl was born, or what colour her hair is, or whose language she dreams in. With them, an hour at a time, she could be simply herself.

In the evenings, when at last her work was done, she would go down to the water alone, and sing to them in her own lost language, and dance on the cool wet sand — and for a little while she would forget how very far she was from home.

IV

The Rose-Gold Slippers

One evening the old man woke from his nap and could not say what had woken him. Then he heard it: singing, drifting up from the river. He rose, and went quietly down through the palms to look — and there was his strange pale servant girl, alone in the last gold light, dancing.

She danced so lightly that her feet seemed scarcely to touch the wet sand, as though the river itself were holding her up and turning her. The old man watched a long while, and something in him was moved. A girl who could dance like that, he thought, ought not to dance barefoot. The next day he sent for the best leatherworker in all of Naucratis and ordered, at no small cost, a pair of slippers made just for her: soft as a second skin, with toes of gilded leather, and dyed the deep blushing rose-gold of a lotus closing at sunset.

Rhodopis had never in her life owned a single beautiful thing. When she slipped them on, she could hardly breathe for the wonder of it. But the other girls saw the gold wink on the foreign girl's feet, and what little kindness they had left curdled into something harder. They hated her now in earnest.

V

Sailed Away Without Her

Now in those days the Pharaoh of all Egypt was a man named Amasis, and that season he sent word the length of the river: he would hold a great gathering at Memphis, his city far upstream — days of feasting and music and dancing, and every soul in the kingdom was welcome to come and stand before their king.

The whole household caught fire with the news. The servant girls washed and oiled their hair, painted their eyes with green malachite, and chattered for days about the dances they would dance before the Pharaoh himself. And Rhodopis, listening, let herself hope — just a little — that she might go too, and dance, and for one day be like anybody else.

But the girls had decided otherwise. On the very morning the boat was to leave, they handed her a heaping mountain of washing and a list of tasks longer than the daylight, and told her, sweet as honey, that of course she might follow them to Memphis — the moment every last chore was finished. Then they stepped into the boat in all their finery and pushed off up the Nile without her. Their laughter carried back across the water long after the white sail had shrunk to a speck and was gone.

VI

The Falcon

Rhodopis did not cry — or not for long. She gathered the washing as she had a thousand times before and carried it down to the river. And because she could not bear to let the mud spoil the one beautiful thing she owned, she slipped off her rose-gold slippers and set them carefully on a flat warm rock, where they would stay clean and dry while she worked.

She was bent over the water, scrubbing, when a shadow swept across the sun. She looked up just in time to see it: a falcon, wings wide and fierce, dropping out of the bright sky. It fell like a thrown spear, closed its talons around one of the rose-gold slippers, and rose again — beating hard, climbing, until it was a dark speck against the blue, and then it was nothing at all.

Rhodopis stood in the shallows with the single remaining slipper clutched to her chest, staring at the empty sky. In Egypt the falcon was the very bird of Horus, the great sky-god — but she did not think of gods. She thought only that the world had reached down and taken from her again, and that the most beautiful thing she had ever been given was gone.

“A thing dropped from the sky
is not dropped by accident.”
— what the Pharaoh said, holding a slipper that had fallen out of heaven into his lap
VII

The Slipper in the Pharaoh's Lap

But the falcon flew true, and far. It rose above the green ribbon of the Nile and followed it upstream, mile after shining mile, all the way to Memphis — and there, at that very hour, the Pharaoh Amasis sat in the open court of his palace, between painted columns shaped like lotus flowers, hearing the troubles and quarrels of his people as a good king should.

The falcon circled once above the throne. Then it opened its talons, and the little rose-gold slipper fell — soft and slow as a drifting leaf — straight into the Pharaoh's lap.

Amasis lifted it and turned it in his hands, this small bright thing dropped out of the empty sky by the bird of the sky-god himself. He did not believe for a moment that it was chance. A sign so strange, he told his startled court, was sent for a reason, and the reason was plain: the woman whose foot this slipper was made for was meant to be his queen. His advisers must have thought him half-mad — to choose a bride by a shoe fallen from a bird, a woman he had never seen, who might be anyone at all in a kingdom of a thousand towns. But Amasis had learned the one thing kings so often forget: when heaven troubles itself to hand you a sign, you do not haggle with it. And so he gave the command. The slipper would be carried to every town and landing in Egypt, up the river and down, and tried on every woman in the land, until the one foot was found that it had been made to fit.

VIII

Found by the Whole of Egypt

For weeks the Pharaoh's men rowed from landing to landing, and at every stop the women of Egypt crowded down to the water to try the rose-gold slipper. Great ladies forced and frowned; merchants' daughters pinched their toes; servant girls held their breath and pushed — and not one of them could make it fit, for it had been shaped for a single foot in all the world.

At last the royal boat came to rest at the old man's house near Naucratis. And the very servant girls who had sailed away laughing now shoved to the front, certain their hour had come. Each in her turn sat and strained and twisted — and each in her turn failed. The Pharaoh's man was already gathering the slipper to go when he caught sight of someone half-hidden behind the others: a fair-haired girl in a wet and faded tunic, trying with all her might to make herself small and unseen.

He called her forward. The other girls laughed out loud — a foreign slave? But the man held out the slipper, and Rhodopis sat, and slid in her foot, and it went on as smoothly as if it had been poured for her. And then, while the whole household stared, she reached into the fold of her tunic and drew out its twin — the slipper she had saved, and clutched, and never let go.

The very thing that had made her an outsider all her life — that she had come from somewhere else, that she did not belong, that she was not like the rest — was now the sign that the sky itself had chosen her. They carried her up the green Nile to Memphis, and the Pharaoh Amasis took the girl who had come down the river, the pale stranger with no one in the world, and made her the queen of all Egypt.

They say she never forgot the river, or the birds, or the long grey years of being no one — and that she made a gentle queen for exactly that reason, because she remembered, better than anyone in the palace, how it felt to be unseen. The foreign girl the other servants would not so much as look at had become the one face in all of Egypt that everyone now turned to see.

This story is old almost past believing. The Greek geographer Strabo wrote it down two thousand years ago — and a real woman named Rhodopis seems to have lived in Egypt even earlier than that, a Greek slave who, the old historians say, became famous and wealthy on the banks of the Nile. It is the oldest Cinderella anyone has ever found: a thousand years older than the French slipper of glass, older than every version you were ever told as a child. The girl who danced by the river was the first of them all.

And the magic, you'll have noticed, was never a fairy godmother. It was a kind old man, a clever bird, and a girl who stayed gentle in a place that gave her every reason not to.

جُحا
Goha, the Wise Fool
Eight short tales of the most beloved fool in the world · who was never really a fool at all
before we begin

Who Is Goha?

In Egypt we call him Goha. Elsewhere in the Arab world he is Juha; the Turks know him as Nasreddin Hodja; the Persians, as Mulla Nasruddin; and his little stories have travelled, over a thousand years, all the way along the old trade roads into China. He is, in plain fact, the most widely loved fool in the world.

And here is his secret: he is not really a fool at all. In tale after tale — and there are thousands of them, each no longer than a breath — Goha shuffles into view on his patient grey donkey, looking like the village simpleton, and says or does something that makes everyone around him roll their eyes. And then, just as you are sure of his foolishness, it turns itself inside out, and it is the proud man, or the greedy man, or the powerful man, who is suddenly left holding the short end of the stick.

His stories are short because he likes them that way. Here are eight of the best. Read them slowly; the laugh and the lesson usually arrive together, half a heartbeat apart.

I

Thanks Be to God

One morning Goha went out to the field and found that his donkey was gone. He looked behind the house and along the canal and up and down the dusty road, asking everyone he met, and the whole village joined the hunt — for a man without his donkey, in those days, was a man without his legs.

At last they found Goha himself standing in the middle of the square, his face turned up to the sky, his hands lifted, saying over and over, "Thanks be to God. Thanks be to God."

His neighbours stared at him. "Goha, have you lost your wits along with your donkey? You have searched all morning, you have found nothing, and here you stand thanking God? What in the world is there to be thankful for?"

"Why," said Goha, lowering his hands and looking at them in mild surprise, "I am thanking God that I was not sitting on the donkey's back when it went missing. Think how much worse it would be. Then I should be lost as well, and who would ever find me?"

II

How Hard It Is to Please Everyone

Goha had a son who fretted endlessly about what other people thought of him. The boy could hardly take a step for worrying whether someone, somewhere, might laugh. So one day Goha decided to cure him, and to let the boy learn the cure for himself. They set off together for the next village, with their donkey, to see what the world had to say.

First the boy rode while Goha walked alongside. They passed a knot of travellers who clicked their tongues: "Look at that — a strong young lad lolling on the donkey while his poor grey-haired father trudges in the dust. Shameful." So at the next bend they swapped, and Goha rode while the boy walked. They passed more people, who frowned just as hard: "Look at that great healthy man riding in comfort while the little one stumbles along behind. Has he no heart?"

So they both climbed onto the donkey together. And the next travellers gasped in horror: "Two grown souls on one poor little beast! Will they break its back? Where is their mercy?" So they both got down and walked beside it, leading it by the rope — and a fresh band of strangers burst out laughing: "Will you look at these two fools, wearing out their own feet beside a perfectly good donkey nobody is riding!"

At his wits' end, Goha and his son hoisted the donkey up onto their shoulders and staggered on. And of course the very next people they met laughed loudest of all, and pointed, and called out that two madmen were carrying a donkey down the public road. Goha set the animal gently back on its feet, dusted off his hands, and turned to his son. "There," he said. "Now you have seen it with your own eyes. Whatever you do, someone will have something to say about it. So you may as well stop listening to all of them, and simply do as you think is right."

III

The Pot That Gave Birth

Goha went to his neighbour one day and asked to borrow a big copper cooking pot, for he had guests coming. The neighbour, who was fond of his possessions, lent it grudgingly. The next morning Goha returned it — and inside the big pot sat a small one. "What is this?" asked the neighbour. "Ah," said Goha, "a happy surprise. Your pot gave birth in the night. The little one is its child, and by every law it belongs to you." The neighbour thought Goha a perfect fool, but he was not such a fool as to argue with a free pot, and he took both, grinning.

A few weeks later Goha came again, and the neighbour — remembering the little pot — handed over the big one at once, with a generous smile this time. But the days went by, and the pot did not come back. At last the neighbour walked over to Goha's house to ask for it.

"My friend," said Goha, with a face full of sorrow, "I have terrible news. Your pot has died."

"Died?" cried the neighbour. "Have you lost your mind? Whoever heard of a pot dying?"

"And yet," said Goha gently, "only last month you had no trouble at all believing that one could be born." The neighbour opened his mouth, and shut it again, and went home with no pot — and a great deal to think about.

IV

The Smell and the Sound

There was a poor man in the town who could afford nothing but dry bread, and each day he would sit to eat it in the doorway of a grand kebab shop, where at least the rich smell of the grilling meat drifting out made his plain bread go down a little easier.

One day the owner of the shop — a hard and grasping man — caught him at it, and seized him by the collar, and demanded that he pay. Pay for what? For the smell of the food, the owner said, which the poor man had been helping himself to, day after day, for free. The poor man had not a coin to his name, and the quarrel grew so loud that at last the two of them were marched before Goha, who was acting that day as the judge.

Goha listened to the whole tale with great seriousness, stroking his beard and nodding. Then he reached into his own purse, took out a single coin, and dropped it onto the stone floor, where it rang out brightly — once, and then again. He turned to the shop-owner. "There. Did you hear that?"

"Of course I heard it," said the owner.

"Good," said Goha. "Then you are paid in full, and the matter is settled. This man enjoyed the smell of your food; you have now enjoyed the sound of his money. A fair price for a fair portion. Court is closed."

V

Goha's Nail

Goha once owned a fine house, but hard times came, and he was forced to sell it. He found a buyer and they agreed a price — and then Goha added one small condition. He would sell the whole house, every room and every stone, save for a single iron nail driven into the wall of the main room. That one nail would remain his, forever, and he would keep the right to come and visit it whenever he pleased. The buyer thought this the harmless whim of a simpleton, and gladly signed.

He soon learned what kind of simpleton he was dealing with. At all hours Goha would arrive to "see to his nail." He came at dawn, while the family slept; he came at supper, and at the middle of feasts; he would hang his wet, dripping cloak upon it and leave it there to dry, or bring a crowd of friends to stand about admiring it. There was no peace in that house from morning until night, for the owner could refuse him nothing — the nail, after all, was Goha's, written plainly in the contract.

In the end the poor man could bear it no longer, and sold the entire house back to Goha for a fraction of what he had paid, simply to be free of him and his nail. To this very day in Egypt, when a person keeps one tiny foothold in order to keep his grip on the whole, we call it Goha's nail — and everyone understands at once exactly what is meant.

VI

Eat, My Robe, Eat

Goha was once invited to a great feast at the house of a wealthy man. But he had come straight from his work, in his oldest and dustiest everyday clothes, and when he arrived the servants looked him up and down, decided he was nobody, and seated him by the door, where the dishes never quite reached and no one troubled to speak to him.

Goha said nothing. He slipped quietly out, went home, and put on his very finest robe — the long, rich, embroidered one he kept for holy days — and came back. And now what a difference! The same servants who had ignored him bowed low and ushered him to the place of honour at the head of the table; the host himself rose to greet him; and the finest dishes were carried over and heaped before him, plate upon plate.

So Goha took the wide hanging end of his sleeve, and dipped it carefully into the bowl of stew, and said, in a voice loud enough for the whole table to hear: "Eat, my robe, eat! Take your fill! For it is plain that it was you who were invited to this feast, and not me at all — so it is only right that you should be the one to dine."

VII

Where the Light Is Better

A friend, passing through the lane one night, came upon Goha down on his hands and knees beneath the glow of the street lantern, patting the dusty ground this way and that, searching for something.

"Goha, what have you lost?"

"My key," said Goha, without looking up. "I have dropped my key."

So the friend got down on his own knees to help, and the two of them crawled and patted and sifted the dust beneath the lamp for a long, long while, and found nothing at all. At last the friend sat back and asked, "Goha — are you quite sure you dropped it here?"

"Here? Oh, no," said Goha, pointing off into the darkness. "I dropped it over there, inside the house."

"Then in the name of all that is sensible," cried the friend, "why are we searching for it out here?"

"Well," said Goha, looking up at him as though it were the most obvious thing in the world, "because the light is so much better here." And the friend laughed — and then, walking home, stopped laughing, and wondered how many of his own troubles he had been hunting for in the easy, well-lit places, instead of the dark corner where he had actually let them fall.

VIII

Teaching the Donkey to Read

Goha's fame for cleverness spread so far that at last it reached the ears of the king — and the king, who did not like to hear of any man being cleverer than himself, decided to set Goha a task that could not be done. "Goha," he commanded, "I have heard you are very wise. So you will teach my donkey to read. And if, in the end, the creature cannot read — I will have your head."

The whole court fell silent, certain Goha was finished. But Goha only bowed low and said, "It is a great honour, Your Majesty. A task like this cannot be rushed, of course — to teach a donkey its letters properly takes time. Grant me ten years, and a good supply of books, and it shall be done."

The king, amused, agreed, and Goha went home with the royal donkey. His friends were waiting, pale with fear. "Goha, are you mad? No donkey on earth will ever read a word! In ten years the king will have your head after all!"

But Goha sat down quite calmly. "Ten years," he said, "is a very long time. Who can say what it holds? In ten years, the king may die. Or I may die. Or — who knows — the donkey may die. And if none of us dies," he added, with a small smile, "well, then we shall simply have to see whether the donkey has learned to read." For the cleverest answer to an impossible command, Goha knew, is almost always to ask, very politely, for more time.

There are a thousand more tales where these came from, and Goha has been telling them for well over a thousand years — first in Arabic, then carried on the backs of travellers into Turkey and Persia and as far as China, gathering new stories at every stop. In Egypt he never really left. When we are lost in a tangle of winding streets, we say we are in beit Goha — Goha's house. When someone keeps a sly little hold on something, we say he has his nail in it. His foolishness became our common sense.

He spent a thousand years playing the fool, so that the rest of us could be a little wiser — and could laugh, at least, while we learned it.

Louliyya, Daughter of Morgan
An Egyptian wonder-tale, told by mothers in the dark · our Rapunzel, with a ghoul for a witch
before we begin

Tales Told After Dark

In Egypt, the oldest stories were not read out of books. They were told, in the dark, by women — by mothers and grandmothers and aunts, in the quiet hour after the lamps were turned low, when the children lay close together and the night pressed soft against the shutters. These were the wonder-tales, and by long custom they were almost always a woman's to tell.

By now the last of the old tellers of Cairo have fallen silent, and the tales live mostly on paper. Here is one of the finest of them. You will know its bones at once — a girl, a tower, a long rope of hair — because, far to the north and centuries later, it would be told again under another name: Rapunzel. But this older, stranger telling keeps two things that the famous version lost along the way. It keeps a real monster. And it keeps a girl who, in the end, does her own rescuing.

I

The Careless Prince

There was once a prince named Yousif, the son of a great king, who had everything a young man could wish for and all the carelessness that so often comes with it. One afternoon, playing at ball in the lane, he sent it crashing into an old woman's water jars and shattered every one, spilling across the dust the water she had carried a long, hot way on her head. And instead of helping her, or begging her pardon, he threw back his head and laughed.

The old woman did not laugh. She straightened, and fixed him with an eye that made the laughter die in his throat, and she said: "Since you find it so funny, Prince Yousif, then laugh at this. May you know no rest, and no sleep, and no peace in your heart, until you have found Louliyya, the daughter of Morgan." And before he could speak, she was gone, as though the lane had swallowed her.

Yousif laughed once more, uneasily — but the name had already lodged in him like a splinter under the skin. By that night he could not eat. By the next he could not sleep. He had never set eyes on this Louliyya, did not know if she was near or far, living or dreamed, and yet he could think of nothing else in the world. He grew so pale and so thin and so wild-eyed that at last he rose from his bed, took a good horse and a little gold, and rode out of his father's kingdom to find her — or to die in the looking.

II

The Tower With No Door

He rode for months, and asked the name of everyone he met, and most had never heard it. But at the very edge of the settled world, where the green fields gave out into grey desert, an old shepherd went pale at the sound of it. "Louliyya, the daughter of Morgan? Turn your horse around, boy, and ride home. Morgan is a ghoul — an eater of men — and the girl is the one treasure he loves, shut away in a tower with no door and no stair, out there where nothing grows and no one goes." Yousif thanked him kindly, and rode straight on toward the place the shepherd had pointed, fearing nothing now but never finding her.

He came upon the tower at dusk: tall and smooth and pale, blank stone all the way up, with no door at the bottom and a single small window at the very top, impossibly high. He could see no way that any living thing got in or out. So he hid himself among the rocks, and waited. And as the last light failed, a great dark shape came striding across the sand — Morgan the ghoul, taller than two tall men, his shoulders like a doorway — and stopped at the foot of the tower and called up in a voice like two stones grinding: "Louliyya! Louliyya! Let down your hair!"

And down from that high window came tumbling a rope of hair, black and shining, long as the tower was tall. The ghoul took hold of it and climbed up, hand over hand, and disappeared inside. Yousif's heart slammed against his ribs. He waited out the whole long night, and at the first grey of morning the ghoul climbed down again and loped off across the dunes to his hunting. Then, and only then, Yousif crept out, stood in the very spot where the monster had stood, pitched his voice as deep as it would go, and called: "Louliyya! Louliyya! Let down your hair!" The hair came pouring down. He gripped it, set his feet to the wall, and climbed.

III

The Girl in the Tower

At the top, in a single round room with one small window, sat the most beautiful girl Yousif had ever seen — and the loneliest. This was Louliyya. She had never in all her life looked on a human face but her father's, and when this stranger climbed in through her window she was more astonished than afraid. And when she understood that he had crossed the whole world to reach her, that he had fallen sick and nearly died for the mere sound of her name, something in her that had been shut away for years quietly came open. They talked the day through, and by the time the light began to fail, each would sooner have died than be parted from the other.

But Louliyya was afraid for him. "You must hide now," she said, "and be as still and silent as a stone. My father is a ghoul. He can smell the blood of a living man the way you can smell bread baking. The moment he is through that window he will know, and he will say so." She helped Yousif fold himself into a great chest in the corner, and shut the lid, and let down her hair. The ghoul came climbing up, and the instant his head rose over the sill his nostrils flared wide. "I smell a human," he rumbled, turning his huge head this way and that. "I smell the scent of the sons of Adam, here in my own tower."

"And who, father," said Louliyya, as sweetly and as calmly as she could, though her heart was pounding, "would ever climb a doorless tower in the middle of the empty desert? It is only the smell of the lamb you carried home for me yesterday, still on my hands." The ghoul grumbled, and sniffed once more, and at last was satisfied. He ate his great supper and lay down and slept. And that night, while he snored like a landslide, Louliyya — who had watched her father work his magic all her life, and learned far more of it than he ever once suspected — began very quietly to gather the few small things that she and Yousif would need.

IV

The Flight

Before the first light, while the ghoul still slept, Louliyya took three things down from her father's high shelf: a wooden comb, a small round mirror, and a long silver pin. Then she and Yousif slid down the rope of her own hair, and the moment their feet touched the sand they ran.

They ran as the sun came up, and they were far out across the dunes when a roar broke behind them that shook the ground under their feet. The ghoul had woken to an empty tower. He came after them like a storm given legs, swallowing the distance in great strides, and when his shadow had very nearly fallen over them, Louliyya flung the comb down behind her. "Be a forest!" she cried — and where the comb struck the sand there sprang up a thicket of thorns, black and dense and high as a house, and the ghoul had to tear at it with his bare hands for an hour, roaring, before he broke through, streaming blood.

He gained on them again, and Louliyya threw down the little mirror. "Be a sea!" — and behind them a wide black water opened from edge to edge of the world, and the ghoul had to run the long way round it, howling with rage. And when at last, lungs burning, they heard his breath hot at their backs a third time, Louliyya turned and threw down the silver pin. "Be a mountain of fire!" — and up roared a wall of flame that climbed to scrape the very sky.

The ghoul was past all reason now. Rather than go around, he hurled his huge body straight at the fire, clawing toward the daughter who had slipped his grip. And the fire closed over him and took him.

V

The Ghoul's Last Word

But a ghoul does not die quietly, and Morgan had one cruelty left. As the flames closed over him, he gathered the last of his magic and the whole black weight of his spite, and he flung a final curse after the two who had escaped him. "If I cannot keep you," he howled, "then you shall not keep each other!"

The curse struck them as they ran. Louliyya felt herself drop to four feet; her cry came out a whimper; she had been turned into a small brown dog. And beside her Yousif shrank and feathered and rose crying into the air — a lark, a little brown singing bird — and before she could so much as bark his name, the wind caught him and carried him up and away over the hills, until he was a speck, and then he was gone.

So the ghoul had his revenge after all. The girl who had outrun him and outwitted him crouched alone in the dust, unable to speak a word. The prince who had crossed the world for her was a bird on the wind, and gone past finding. And between the two of them now lay the whole wide and pathless world.

VI

What Louliyya Did

But Louliyya had not run across the world and burned a ghoul to be undone by his last spiteful breath. As a dog she had no voice — but she still had her wits, and her memory, and four good legs. And so she set herself a single task: to reach the kingdom Yousif had told her of, on that first long day in the tower, when they had traded their whole lives in a single afternoon.

For months the little dog travelled, footsore and half-starved, sleeping in ditches and living on scraps, until at last she came to the gates of the king's own city. She slipped inside, and made her way through the streets to the palace, and there she lay down at the feet of the queen — who was Yousif's own mother, and who had wept for her lost son every single day that he had been gone. The queen looked down into the eyes of the thin brown stray, and saw in them something that was not a dog at all: something human, and grieving, and straining to speak. She could not have said why, but she gathered the creature up, and warmed it, and fed it from her own hand, and spoke to it gently, and loved it. And kindness freely given, in the old tales, is the one power on earth that no ghoul's curse can stand against. On the third night, as the queen stroked the little dog's head and murmured aloud that she only wished the poor thing could tell her its sorrow — the spell tore, and broke. The dog rose up a girl. There stood Louliyya, in her own true shape, weeping, and pouring out the whole long tale.

Then Louliyya did the last thing, and the hardest. She walked out from the palace into the hills, and she called — softly, in the old way her father's magic had taught her — and called, and called, until out of the wide grey sky a single small lark came spiralling down, and lit, trembling, upon her open palm. She lifted him to her heart. She bent her head and wept over him, and her warm tears fell on his feathers and washed away the very last of the ghoul's curse. The feathers loosened and fell; and there, blinking and thin and astonished and wholly alive, stood Yousif — held in the two hands of the girl who had crossed the world twice over to save him.

Tales like this one were the great treasure of Egyptian women — carried only in memory, told aloud in the dark for hundreds of years, and almost never written down, until a folklorist named Hasan El-Shamy gathered a basketful of them, Louliyya among them, just before the last of the old tellers fell silent. The version the whole world knows now, Rapunzel, kept the tower and the long bright hair. But somewhere on its long road north it lost the ghoul, and it lost something else as well: in this older telling, when the night is at its very darkest, it is not a prince who climbs to the rescue.

She was shut in a tower by a monster, and turned into a dog by his curse — and still, when all was lost, it was Louliyya who did the saving.

الشاطر حسن
El-Shater Hassan
An Egyptian wonder-tale of the underdog who wins by wit and a good heart, not by strength
before we begin

The Clever One

In Arabic, el-shater means the clever one — the quick, the resourceful, the sharp of mind. And in a hundred Egyptian tales, the hero who carries that name is always the same kind of soul: the small one, the overlooked one, the boy nobody bothers to notice, who wins in the end not because he is the strongest or the richest or the best-born, but because he is the cleverest and the kindest. He is the hero Egyptian children have always cheered for, because he is the one who looks the most like them.

This is one of his tales. There is a poor boy in it, and a magic horse, and a sultan's garden, and a whole court of proud men who learn, too late, never to laugh at the quiet one in the corner.

I

The Scrawny Colt

When Hassan's father died, he left almost nothing behind. The good fields and the fat animals went, somehow, to louder and greedier relatives, and what was left for Hassan, the youngest and the gentlest, was a single scrawny colt — a thin, knock-kneed, sorry-looking thing that no one else would take. The relatives laughed as they handed it over. "There is your inheritance, clever boy. Much good may it do you."

But Hassan did not complain, and he did not despair. He took the little colt home, and he fed it from his own small share, and brushed it, and spoke to it kindly every single day, and slept beside it on cold nights so it would not be afraid. And under all that patient care the sorry colt began, slowly, to change — to fill out, and gleam, and grow, until it stood taller and finer and swifter than any horse in the district.

And one night, as Hassan lay against its warm flank under the stars, the horse turned its great head and spoke. "Hassan," it said, in a low and gentle voice, "you took me when I was nothing, and you were kind to me when no one was kind to you. I am no ordinary horse, as you have guessed. Wherever you go, I will carry you, and help you, and stand by you — for just so long as your heart stays as good as it is tonight. Now. Shall we go and see the world?"

Hassan threw his arms around the horse's neck and laughed aloud for joy — not because he had been handed a fortune, but because, after a whole life of being last and least and laughed at, he finally had a friend. They set out together the very next morning, the poor boy and the magical horse, down the long white road toward whatever it was the world had been quietly saving up for him.

II

The Gardener's Boy

Hassan and his horse came at last to a great city, ruled by a powerful sultan who had three daughters. Hassan did not march up to the palace gates and boast, as another young man might have. He hid his magnificent horse safely outside the walls, put on the plainest clothes he owned, and went and asked the sultan's old gardener for the lowest work there was.

So Hassan became the gardener's boy. He hauled water, and turned the heavy wheel that raised it from the well, and carried dung, and dug, and weeded under the hot sun from dawn to dark, for almost no wages at all. The grand people of the court swept past him without a glance, as one walks past a tool leaning against a wall. To every fine lord and lady in that city, he was nobody — a ragged, dirt-streaked nothing, bent over the flowerbeds. And Hassan, who knew exactly who and what he was, only smiled to himself, and kept his secret, and waited.

The work was hard and the days were long, and many a young man would have turned sour at being treated like dirt by people not half his worth. But Hassan kept his temper sweet and his eyes open. He learned the ways of the palace and the moods of the court, and bided his time without bitterness. And the only soul in all that place who ever looked at him twice was the sultan's youngest daughter, who would sometimes watch the quiet gardener's boy from her high window, and wonder — without quite knowing why — what it was that he seemed to be waiting for.

III

The Knight Nobody Knew

One day the old gardener went off to visit his family and pray, and left Hassan alone to mind the garden. The moment he was gone, Hassan slipped out to where his horse was hidden, and the horse breathed on him — and in a heartbeat the ragged gardener's boy was gone. In his place stood a knight in armour that shone like the sun on water, mounted on the most magnificent horse in the world.

And Hassan rode. He rode through the sultan's garden at a glorious gallop, the turf flying, the armour blazing, a young hero out of a song — and high above, at her latticed window, the sultan's youngest daughter looked down and went very still, and lost her heart entirely to the shining stranger she did not know.

Then, before anyone else could come and see, Hassan wheeled the horse back behind the wall, breathed once, and was the ragged gardener's boy again, on his knees in the dirt among the trampled flowers. When the old gardener came home and saw the wreck of his beds and cried out in dismay, Hassan only shrugged and said some wild riders must have come through. And from her window, the youngest princess — who had seen everything, and understood more than anyone guessed — said not one word to give him away.

IV

The Golden Apple

Not long after, the sultan decided it was time his three daughters were married. He gathered all the noble young men of the kingdom in the great square — princes and viziers' sons and rich men's heirs, all in their finest — and he gave each of his daughters a golden apple, and told them to throw it, each in her turn, at the man she chose for her husband.

The eldest threw her apple, and it struck the son of the grand vizier; the people cheered. The second threw hers, and it struck the son of the high judge; the people cheered again. Then the youngest stepped forward — and she lifted her golden apple, and she looked past every prince and every lord and every glittering young man in that square, and she threw it clear over all their heads, so that it came down and landed, of all places, on the ragged gardener's boy standing forgotten at the very back.

A gasp went up, and then a roar of laughter. The gardener's boy! The whole court howled. The sultan went purple with shame and fury, and would have called it a mistake — but the princess only walked the length of the square, through all the laughing nobles, and stood herself quietly at Hassan's side. Her father, too angry to look at her, cast them both out of the palace to live in a broken-down hovel at the edge of the city, and washed his hands of his foolish youngest daughter and her dirt-streaked husband.

And so they lived, the princess and the gardener's boy, in a leaning little house with a leaking roof, poor as two sparrows — and happier, for all that, than her sisters in their fine apartments. For Hassan was gentle and merry and kind, and his wife had never once doubted what she had seen from her window, and was content to wait beside him for the world to catch up to the truth. She did not yet know about the horse. But she knew her husband, and that, she had decided, was enough.

V

The Cure No One Else Could Find

A year went by. And then the old sultan fell gravely ill — so ill that all his physicians gave him up for lost. There was, they said at last, only one cure on earth that might save him: the milk of a wild lioness, fetched from the far country where the lions roam. It was as good as a death sentence, for what man could walk up to a lioness and milk her and live?

The two proud sons-in-law saddled their fine horses and rode off in a great show of confidence to find it. And Hassan, in his hovel, kissed his wife, slipped out to his magic horse, and was there and back before the others had crossed the first range of hills. In his shining armour, on his magnificent steed, he rode into the lion-country, and by the horse's magic the great lionesses lay down before him gentle as house cats, and he filled a flask with their milk.

It was a place no ordinary man ever came back from — a wilderness of dry golden hills where the great cats moved through the grass like fire, and the bones of bolder men lay bleaching white in the sun. But Hassan came and went through it like an honoured guest, for the horse's magic went before him; and where he passed, even the lionesses only blinked their amber eyes and let him by.

He was riding home when he met his two sons-in-law, footsore and frightened and empty-handed, who did not know the splendid stranger for the gardener's boy they despised. They begged him for a little of the lioness's milk. "Gladly," said Hassan. "But everything has its price. You may have some milk — common goat's milk, that is, which is all I will give you — and the price is this: that I set my seal upon you, here, on the skin of your backs, to mark the bargain." The proud men were so desperate, and so sure no one would ever know, that they bared their backs and let the disguised Hassan brand each of them with the mark of his ring. Then they hurried home with their useless goat's milk, well pleased with themselves.

VI

The Boy at the Back of the Square

Back at the palace, the two sons-in-law presented their milk with great ceremony — and it did nothing at all. The sultan sank lower, and the whole court despaired. And then, quietly, the youngest princess came forward and said that her husband, the gardener's boy, had brought something that might help. The nobles sniggered, even then. But Hassan stepped up and gave the sultan the true milk of the lioness — and within a day the old man sat up, and ate, and was well.

The sultan, overjoyed and ashamed, asked how a ragged gardener's boy had found the one cure his proudest lords could not. So Hassan told the whole story. And when the two sons-in-law loudly called him a liar, Hassan smiled and asked only that the marks on their backs be looked at. They were bared before the court — and there, burned into the skin of each, was the seal of Hassan's ring. The milk they had brought, he said, was the goat's milk he had sold them, for the price of that very mark.

Then Hassan walked out to the courtyard and whistled, and his great horse came, and breathed on him — and the gardener's boy stood transformed before them all in shining armour: the very knight the youngest princess had seen from her window, all that time ago. The court that had laughed twice now fell utterly silent. And the sultan came down from his place, and took the hand of the boy from the back of the square, and led him up to sit beside him as the wisest and worthiest man in the kingdom.

As for the youngest princess, who alone had seen the truth while it was still dressed in rags — she was honoured from that day above both her sisters, for having chosen, with her eyes seemingly shut, what the whole proud court could not see with theirs wide open.

El-Shater Hassan has worn a hundred different faces in a hundred different tellings, all across Egypt — sometimes a prince in hiding, sometimes a fisherman's son, sometimes just a poor boy with a clever head and a kind heart. But he is always, underneath, the same: the one the world counts for nothing, who turns out to be worth more than all the proud men put together. He never wins by being the strongest. He wins by being the cleverest, and the kindest, and by waiting — without bitterness — for the day the truth comes out.

And in Egypt we have loved him for a thousand years, because he is proof of the thing we most want to be true: that the quiet one at the back of the square may be the best of everyone there.

أرض النفاق
The Land of Hypocrisy
A modern Egyptian satire · from the 1949 novel by Youssef El-Sebai

A Shop That Sold Virtues

Every other story on this shelf is old, and has no single author; it was polished smooth by a thousand mouths over a thousand years. This last one is different. It is barely older than your grandparents, and one man sat down and wrote it: an Egyptian named Youssef El-Sebai, an army officer who became one of the country's most loved storytellers. He published it in 1949, in Cairo, three years before the old kingdom fell.

It is a small, funny, biting daydream of a book, and it begins with a question so simple a child could ask it, and so sharp that grown men squirm: what would happen to a person who tried to be truly good, right here, in the world as it actually is? El-Sebai answered it by imagining a shop. An ordinary little shop on an ordinary Cairo street, that sold, over the counter and by the measure, virtues. Here is the tale he told.

I

The Morality Shop

There was once a clerk, a capable and unremarkable man, who worked his quiet government job and went home each night to his quiet house. One evening, after an unusually heavy supper, he was walking off the meal through streets he thought he knew, when he came upon a shop he had never seen before: small, dim, half-forgotten, with a faded sign that read, of all the impossible things, Morals and Virtues — Wholesale and Retail.

He laughed out loud. Surely it was a joke, or the name of some grocer with a sense of humour. But the door was open, so he stepped inside — and there, behind a worn wooden counter, stood an old man with a gentle, tired face, surrounded by shelves of sacks and jars, each one neatly labelled. Courage. Chivalry. Honesty. Loyalty. Mercy. A great brass scale hung from the ceiling for weighing them out.

"You sell these?" asked the clerk, still grinning. "By the pound?" "By the pound, by the dram, however much you can carry," said the old man, with no humour at all. "Though I will tell you plainly, my son, it is a poor trade. These good sacks here sit and gather dust for years. The shelf that empties fastest is the one behind you." The clerk turned. Behind him, nearly bare, scraped clean by eager hands, were the bins marked Flattery. Cowardice. Lies. And Hypocrisy.

The old man watched him take it in. "You look surprised. But where else would a man go to buy such things? You cannot inherit courage, and you cannot study mercy out of a book. People like to imagine the virtues come free, that they lie about inside us from birth, waiting to be picked up and used. They do not. They are dear, and they are heavy to carry, and most men, once they hear the price, decide they would rather travel light." He gestured at his dusty shelves with something between pride and grief. "I have kept this shop forty years. I could close it tomorrow, open a stall of flattery in its place, and retire fat inside a season. But somebody, somewhere, has to keep selling the real thing, even at a loss. Now then. What will it be?"

II

A Measure of Courage

The clerk, half believing and half playing along, decided to try it. "Give me some courage, then," he said. The old man sighed as if relieved to move any of his good stock at all, weighed out a small bright measure of it, wrapped it carefully, and named a modest price. The clerk paid, and pocketed his courage, and walked home wondering what on earth had come over him.

But it was real. The next morning he felt it working in him like a strong drink. At the office, when his superior took credit for another man's work and then blamed a junior clerk for his own mistake, our hero did the thing he had wanted to do for twenty quiet years: he stood up, and said, plainly and politely and in front of everyone, exactly what was true.

It was a catastrophe. The whole office froze in horror, as though he had taken off his clothes. His superior went white, then purple. By noon he had been marked as a troublemaker, a madman, a danger; his colleagues edged away from him in the corridor as if courage were a contagious disease. He went home shaken, and found, to his surprise, that he did not regret it. That, he was learning, is what courage does. It is also, he was learning, why so few people in that city had bought any.

That evening an old friend took him quietly aside, the way one speaks to the gravely ill, and explained the facts of life as if to a child. One did not say such things, the friend murmured, not because they were false, but precisely because they were true. The truth was a loaded thing; a wise man kept the safety on. Everyone in that office had always known exactly what their superior was. That had never been the question. The only question, the friend sighed, was whether you were fool enough to say it out loud.

III

An Honest Man

If courage had cost him so much, the clerk thought, perhaps the trouble was that he had bought only one virtue and not the rest. So he went back to the shop, and this time he bought generously: chivalry, loyalty, and a good full measure of honesty. He would not do things by halves. He would be, from this day, a wholly good man.

It nearly destroyed him. Honesty, set loose in that city, was a wrecking ball. When friends asked him how they looked, or whether their poems were any good, he told them, kindly, the truth, and they never forgave him. When his wife asked the small daily questions that married people ask, expecting the small daily lies that keep a household warm, she got instead clean honesty, and the house went cold. His chivalry got him cheated; his loyalty got him used; his honesty got him shunned. Within weeks the good and capable clerk had nearly lost his job, very nearly lost his wife, and was being spoken of, in lowered voices, as a man who ought perhaps to be quietly put away somewhere, for his own good and everyone else's.

For this, he came to understand, is the deadly secret of the land of hypocrisy: it is not that the people in it cannot tell good from bad. It is that they have all silently agreed that the good is unliveable, and the man who insists on practising it out loud is not a saint but a public nuisance.

His wife wept, and not because he had done anything she could name as wrong. She wept because she no longer knew how to live beside a man who would not tell her she looked well when she was worn out, or that the dinner was wonderful when it had plainly burned. The small kind lies, she tried to explain to him, were not really lies at all; they were a kind of mortar, the soft grey stuff that lets people live pressed close against one another without grinding each other away to nothing. He had gone and bought himself a hammer, and now he walked through the house knocking the mortar out from between the bricks, and stood there honestly astonished that the walls had started to crack.

IV

The Shopkeeper's Sorrow

Bruised in body and spirit, the clerk went back one last time to the little shop, half hoping the old man would take his virtues back and refund his peace of mind. He found the shopkeeper sitting among his dusty, unsold sacks of goodness, looking sadder than ever.

"You warned me," the clerk admitted. "Why do you stay open? You said yourself nobody buys the good." "Somebody must keep the door open," said the old man simply. "If the shop ever closes, then the virtues are not merely unsold. They are gone. And besides," he added, lowering his voice, "I have something here I have never sold, and never will, except perhaps once, to the whole world at once." From beneath the counter he drew a small sealed jar of fine, glowing powder. "The pure essence of morals, distilled. A single pinch of this in a river would be enough to turn an entire people honest overnight. I have often dreamed of doing it. And I have never quite dared, because I do not know if a people built on hypocrisy could survive the shock of suddenly seeing one another, and themselves, exactly as they are."

The clerk looked at the little jar for a long time. He thought of his frozen office and his cold house and his vanished friends, and of how easy his life had been, and how warm, back when he too had quietly traded in flattery and small forgiving lies. And a terrible, tempting thought came to him: what if he simply emptied the whole jar into the Nile, the river the entire city drank from, and forced the cure on every last soul at once, whether they wished it or not?

V

The River

He stood on the bank of the Nile that night with the little jar in his hand, and the great dark river slid past, carrying the moon on its back. One pinch, the old man had said. The whole city drank this water. He could make them all honest before breakfast. He could end the loneliness of being the only good man in the land of hypocrisy by simply abolishing the hypocrisy.

What exactly happened next, the storyteller tells slyly, and not quite the same way twice. In one breath it is the powder of virtue that goes into the river, and for a day the city is seized by an astonishing fit of honesty, with marvels and disasters in equal measure. In another, it is the authorities themselves who step in, alarmed, and quietly pour the opposite sack into the water instead, the great surplus of hypocrisy that no shop could ever sell out, so that it dissolves into the current and runs into every tap and every field and every cup of tea, into the very bodies of the people, until hypocrisy is simply in the water they are made of, and the land is named, once and for all, after it.

Either way, the good does not win easily, and the river keeps its secret.

VI

Waking on the Sofa

And then the clerk opened his eyes — and found himself stretched out on the sofa in his own front room, the heavy supper sitting like a stone beneath his ribs, the lamp still burning. There had been no shop. There had been no scale, no glowing jar, no river by moonlight. He had dozed off and dreamed the whole thing.

He sat up, rubbing his eyes, ready to laugh it all away as the nonsense of a full stomach. And he could not. Because the strange thing about the dream, he found, was that nothing in it had actually been untrue. The shop was invented; the office was not. The magic scale was a fancy; the cold that fell over a room when a man told the simple truth was something he had felt a thousand times, wide awake. The fantasy had been a thin curtain, and behind it stood his own ordinary city, exactly as it was. Which, he decided, was the most unsettling thing of all.

He lay back down and stared at the ceiling and did the sum honestly, the way the dream had taught him. Tomorrow he could walk into the office and tell the truth, and lose nearly everything, and be right. Or he could say good morning, and admire his superior's dreadful judgement, and keep his job and his friends and his warm house, and be exactly like everyone else. The shop had been make-believe. But the choice it set in front of him was real, and it was waiting for him at breakfast, and it would be waiting again the morning after that, and the morning after that. And that, he understood at last, was the land of hypocrisy. It was not a place on any map. It was a small, quiet decision, handed to every person alive, every single day.

Youssef El-Sebai wrote The Land of Hypocrisy in 1949, and dressed his sharpest social criticism in the gentle clothes of a dream so that everyone could laugh and no one could quite take offence. It is remembered as one of the funniest and most quietly furious things written in the last years of the old Egyptian kingdom, and Egyptians have loved it ever since, on the page and later on the screen, partly because they keep recognising their own world in it. This telling is my own; the shop, the clerk, and the wonderful idea behind them are his.

He set his whole tale inside a dream — and then admitted, with a wink, that the truths in it had quite overwhelmed the make-believe.
